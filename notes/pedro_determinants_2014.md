# On the Determinants of Cooperation in Infinitely Repeated Games: A Survey | Project Pillar: Literature Review
**Date:** May 2026
**Status:** Peer-Reviewed Literature

## 🎯 Executive Summary
This survey analyzes a meta-dataset of laboratory experiments on infinitely repeated prisoners' dilemma games to identify the determinants of cooperation. It finds that while infinite repetition and equilibrium feasibility encourage cooperation, high rates of cooperation only emerge when game parameters make cooperation robust to strategic uncertainty.

## 🔑 Key Arguments & Findings
* **Finding 1:** In repeated games, theory often predicts multiple equilibria (multiplicity of equilibria), meaning that cooperation and defection can both be supported; empirical/experimental results help resolve this by showing cooperation is far more likely when it can be supported in equilibrium.
* **Finding 2:** Simply having cooperative equilibria is insufficient; high cooperation rates are only sustained when parameters (like the discount factor or continuation probability) make cooperation robust to strategic uncertainty (i.e. risk dominance or payoff differences from deviation).

## 🛠️ Methodological Notes / Technical Specifications
* **Methodology:** Meta-analysis using a compiled meta-dataset of laboratory experiments on infinitely repeated prisoners' dilemma games.
* **Parameters Analyzed:** Discount factor ($\delta$), continuation probability, imperfect monitoring, changing partners, and personal characteristics of subjects.

## 💡 Personal Insights & Open Questions
* **Potential Application:** The insights on repeated game dynamics and cooperation under strategic uncertainty can be applied to game-theoretic modeling of transactive energy markets. Specifically, when EGoT devices negotiate energy trades or offer flexibility services repeatedly, the discount factor (frequency of market clearance) and monitoring accuracy determine if cooperative, grid-supporting behavior can be sustained.
* **Gap/Next Step:** The survey focuses on classical prisoners' dilemma games with two options (cooperate/defect). How do these findings on cooperation translate to continuous-strategy spaces (e.g. continuous power adjustments or bid curves) and complex, multi-agent systems with network constraints?
